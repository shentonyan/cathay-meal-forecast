1. Executive summary

Airlines throw away a large share of the food they load. That food cannot be recycled cheaply, because international catering waste usually has to be incinerated or buried. The most useful lever is therefore to load the right number of the right meals in the first place. This brief looks at whether an AI meal-forecasting and pre-order system is a credible, differentiated hackathon idea for Cathay Pacific. The evidence says yes, for four reasons.

  • The problem is large and measurable. IATA audits put cabin waste at 0.94 kg per passenger, or 3.6 million tonnes in 2023 [1]. About a third of that waste is untouched food and beverages, worth around US$6 billion a year [2].
  • The approach works in production. KLM's AI tool predicts how many booked passengers will actually fly. A three-month analysis found 63% less food wasted than when catering for every booked passenger [3].
  • The academic literature leaves a gap. Published models are mostly trained on synthetic data. None of them joins up pre-order signals, per-dish forecasting and an explicit loading-optimisation rule.
  • Cathay already has the foundations but not the forecasting layer. Choose My Meal already feeds consumption insights back into catering [4]. However, it covers only First and Business [5].
Proposed pitch: extend pre-ordering to every cabin, reward it with Asia Miles, and use the pre-order signal plus a gradient-boosting forecast to set per-flight, per-dish load quantities with a newsvendor rule. Success means "fewer meals in the bin, the same chance of getting your first choice."

2. The problem: scale and economics of inflight food waste

Estimates differ by methodology, but they all point the same way. IATA's 2024 preliminary audits of flights from Singapore found food and beverage made up 65% of cabin waste, and untouched meals alone were 18% of all waste [6]. An earlier IATA estimate valued the inflight catering market at US$20 billion, of which about US$4 billion of food and drink was incinerated or landfilled [7]. Industry reporting puts the untouched share at 18–20% of catering materials, roughly 700,000 tonnes a year [8].

Why recycling cannot solve it

EU Regulation 1069/2009 requires international catering waste to be incinerated or buried in an authorised landfill [9]. The UK classifies it as a high-risk Category 1 animal by-product [10]. As a result, industry analysts describe reducing the load before departure as the only practical way to cut this waste stream [8].

The hidden fuel cost

Every surplus meal is also carried weight. IATA's rule of thumb is that each extra tonne burns about 30 kg of fuel per flight hour [11]. A model cited by Airbus estimates that removing 1 kg of unnecessary load from an aircraft flying 1,825 flights a year saves about US$4,210 a year in fuel [8]. That means forecasting saves money twice: on meal production and on fuel.

3. What the academic literature says

The literature is small and fragmented, but it agrees on the main drivers. Recent work falls into three groups: machine-learning prediction, operations-research loading models, and behavioural studies.

Other work supports the main design choices. A study using data from a Sri Lankan caterer serving 14 airlines found that demand uncertainty strongly increases production waste [12]. The LIFE+ Zero Cabin Waste project audited 145 flights, split by flight time and cabin class [12]. Tofalli et al. suggest that letting passengers opt in or out of meals at booking could reduce waste [12]. The DGLR paper also cites IATA on the value of meal pre-selection [13]. For a realistic target, a restaurant-sector benchmark found ML forecasting reduced wasted meals by 14–52% and unmet demand by 3–16% [12].

Most predictive features

Across these studies the same features recur: passenger count and late changes in load factor; route and passenger nationality mix, which drives preferences such as vegetarian share [13]; flight duration and cabin; dish type and how popular it has been historically; and season and departure time.

The gap Cathay can fill

Real-world catering consumption data is described as practically non-existent in the research community [13]. Pre-ordering research, such as Ernits et al.'s automation concept [17], and forecasting research have developed separately. No study we found uses pre-orders as a model input, forecasts per-dish shares, and then optimises load quantities under a service-level constraint. Nor has anyone studied a multicultural Asian hub such as HKG. The proposed system fills this gap.

Two lessons stand out. First, the largest verified result, at KLM, comes from forecasting rather than from pre-ordering. Pre-order programmes are now common, but airlines publish almost no outcome numbers for them. Second, intellectual-property risk exists in this space: Emirates is being sued over the technology behind its meal pre-ordering [23]. A hackathon prototype should therefore stress its own modelling approach rather than the pre-order interface. Results for Singapore Airlines, Qantas, ANA, Etihad and the major caterers were not found in this research.

5. Cathay Pacific today: strengths and gaps

What already exists

  • Choose My Meal. Ordering opens 10 days before departure and closes 24 hours before, on eligible long-haul and regional flights [5]. Cathay Signatures pre-orders are available on selected flights from Hong Kong [5].
  • In-house catering scale. Cathay's catering arm, now Cathay Dining, caters for more than 45 airlines and produced about 83,800 meals a day in 2017 [24]. A forecasting engine could therefore serve third-party airlines as well, which is a possible commercial extension.
  • End-of-life handling. The Feeding Hong Kong partnership has redistributed 2,250 tonnes of food since 2014 [25]. Cathay Dining's 2025 waste-segregation system raised food-waste recycling capacity by 200% [26].
  • A track record on cabin sustainability targets. Single-use plastic items fell to 1.7 per passenger in 2025, with 1.5 expected by mid-2026 [27]. Cathay already sets per-passenger cabin KPIs and reports against them.

Where the opportunity is

Our inference from public material is that Cathay's current approach is retrospective: it uses past consumption to adjust later loads. Cathay's waste work is also concentrated at end of life, through donation and recycling. Pre-ordering does not reach Premium Economy or Economy, where most passengers sit. We found no public Asia Miles incentive for pre-ordering or skipping a meal, and no published food-waste KPI. With more than 36 million Group passengers in 2025, up 27% [28], even small per-passenger gains add up. The idea also fits the hackathon's stated purpose of bringing external technology and ideas into the company [29].

6. Recommended modelling approach

We recommend a two-layer architecture: first predict a demand distribution for each dish, then make a loading decision from it.

Layer 1: per-flight, per-dish demand forecast

  • Gradient boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost) predicts each dish's share of choices per flight and cabin. Inputs are route, departure time, block time, season, load factor and its last-24-hour change, nationality mix, historical leftovers and the pre-order count. This follows the random-forest results in the literature with a stronger, quick-to-train learner.
  • Passenger-level choice model (multinomial logit) for known Asia Miles members, aggregated to give a flight-level mean and variance, then blended with the gradient-boosting output.
  • Pre-orders as hard demand. Pre-ordered meals are fixed; the model only forecasts the remaining, non-pre-ordered passengers. Every extra pre-order therefore narrows the uncertainty.

Layer 2: newsvendor loading rule

The optimal load Q* satisfies P(D ≤ Q*) = Cu/(Cu+Co), where Cu is the cost of running out and Co the cost of over-catering [30]. In practice the load is the demand quantile at that critical ratio [31]. The rule deliberately leans toward whichever mistake is cheaper [32]. For an airline, running out of a passenger's first choice carries a goodwill cost, so the demo should let users set a target first-choice availability, for example 95%, and show the trade-off curve between waste and service.

Data for the demo

Public catering data does not exist, so the demo should follow the DGLR approach and use realistic synthetic data. The flight skeleton (HKG routes, aircraft types, schedules) can come from OpenSky, which offers real-time APIs, historical archives and research datasets [33]. Dish shares can be drawn from distributions by route, time of day and nationality mix. In the pitch, it should be said plainly that Cathay's own load and return data would replace the synthetic data.

7. KPIs and an illustrative ROI model

KPIDefinition
Over-catering rate(meals loaded − meals consumed) / meals loaded
Waste weightleftover meals × average meal weight (kg)
First-choice availabilitypassengers receiving their first-choice dish / total passengers
Fuel savedweight removed (kg) × 0.03 × block hours
CO₂ avoidedfuel saved × 3.16
Pre-order uptakepre-ordered passengers / eligible passengers

The 0.03 factor comes from IATA's rule of 30 kg of fuel per tonne per hour, cited above. The 3.16 kg CO₂ per kg of jet fuel is a standard emissions factor that we did not separately source. Traditional cost-of-weight estimates put the penalty at 2.5–5% of the carried weight per flight hour [34].

Illustrative estimate (assumptions, not sourced figures). Assume about 25 million passengers a year on full-service Cathay Pacific flights. The 36 million Group total includes low-cost HK Express. Applying the IATA ratios (0.94 kg of waste per passenger × 18% untouched meals) gives about 0.17 kg of untouched food per passenger, or about 4,200 t a year. A conservative 30% reduction, well below KLM's 63%, removes about 1,270 t of food a year. Assuming an average block time of 5 hours, that also avoids about 190 t of fuel and 600 t of CO₂. At an assumed 0.5 kg and US$5 per meal, the food saving is about 2.5 million meals, or roughly US$12–13 million a year. Cathay's real meal costs and fleet data would replace every assumption here. The demo exposes each one as a slider.

One more lever is uptake. Fewer than 5% of air passengers are believed to pre-order meals, possibly as few as 2% [35]. Without pre-orders, inventory is still packed, loaded and flown at cost [35]. KLM opened pre-ordering on flights over 75 minutes from October 2026 [36]. Research on Finnair frames pre-ordered meals as improving experience, reducing waste and adding revenue at the same time [37].

8. Demo blueprint and pitch strategy

Three-screen web demo

  1. Passenger view: "Choose My Meal for everyone." A mobile-style screen for an Economy passenger on CX 251 HKG→LHR. The passenger picks a dish or chooses "skip meal", earns Asia Miles, and sees the CO₂ their choice saves. A "popular dish may run out" nudge appears, in line with the social-norm findings in the behavioural literature.
  2. Catering planner view: flight forecast. For a selected flight, show bookings, expected no-shows, pre-orders, and the per-dish forecast distribution. Display the recommended load (newsvendor) next to the legacy rule of "load factor × fixed ratio". A first-choice availability slider lets the user watch the load change.
  3. Network dashboard: impact. Annualised meals saved, tonnes of food, fuel, CO₂ and US$ across the HKG network. Inputs are adjustable (uptake %, cost per meal, block hours), and a waste-vs-service trade-off chart is included.

Pitch storyline (about 3 minutes)

(1) Hook: about a third of cabin waste is food nobody touched, and the law says it must be burned. (2) Insight: you cannot recycle your way out of this; you have to forecast. KLM has shown a 63% cut. (3) Cathay advantage: Choose My Meal, Cathay Dining's scale, Asia Miles, and real data that academics do not have. (4) Live demo of the three screens. (5) Impact: thousands of tonnes and millions of dollars, with stated assumptions. (6) Roadmap: pilot on a few HKG long-haul routes, then all cabins, then Cathay Dining's third-party airline clients.

Limitations and open questions

  • Cabin-level meal costs, Cathay's daily flight count, fleet size and long-haul share were not found in public sources.
  • Cathay publishes no food-waste tonnage. The ROI above relies on IATA averages.
  • Uplift from Asia Miles incentives is a hypothesis. It should be presented as an A/B test to run, not as a result.
  • Most academic models are trained on synthetic data, so real-world accuracy is unproven at Cathay's scale.
